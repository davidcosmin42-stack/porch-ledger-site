How to analyze a rental property deal

Underwrite a long-term rental before you love the street: cash-on-cash return, cap rate, DSCR, and a stress test that still works if vacancy or the interest rate is wrong.

This is an underwriting guide, not investment, tax, or legal advice, and not a loan offer. Figures are only as good as the inputs. Confirm taxes, a fresh insurance quote, rent comps, and the interest rate before you show a sheet to a lender or a partner.

The most expensive mistake in a small portfolio is buying the second door because the first one has been quiet.

A pretty street or a seller’s “rent is $X” is not an analysis. To analyze a rental property deal you have to underwrite it as if you did not already love it: what cash actually goes in, what the property might earn after real expenses, whether that income covers the mortgage, and what happens if vacancy or the interest rate is wrong. The formulas are ordinary. The discipline is in the inputs, the reserves, and the stress test.

Start with real inputs, not a pretty flyer

Every later number is downstream of a short list. If these are guesses, the rest of the sheet is decoration.

  • Purchase price, closing costs, and any rehab you will fund before the unit can be rented
  • The loan — or cash, if there is no loan — including the rate, term, and down payment
  • Expected rent, from comps, not only from the seller
  • Property taxes as they are, or as they will be after a reassessment, not last year’s leftover figure
  • A fresh insurance quote for a rental, not the owner-occupied premium
  • Vacancy, ordinary maintenance, and a capital-expenditure reserve you can defend

Keep assumptions in their own cells, separate from calculated values, so you can change vacancy or the rate without rebuilding the sheet. That is what makes a sensitivity grid possible later.

What “cash invested” should include

Cash-on-cash return is only honest if the denominator is honest. Total cash invested is not just the down payment. Add closing costs and the rehab you will write a check for at the start. If you ignore those, every return looks better than the deal you actually bought. On a cash purchase, the whole price plus those costs is the cash you put in.

Net operating income, then cap rate

Net operating income (NOI) is income minus ordinary operating expenses. Income should be rent after a vacancy allowance, plus any other operating income you can actually defend. Operating expenses typically include taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities you still pay, property management (model it even if you self-manage, if you want the deal to still work when you hire it out), and similar costs of keeping the doors open.

NOI does not include loan principal, and it usually should not swallow capital improvements — a new roof, a replacement HVAC — as if they were a typical month’s repair. Those still use cash. Put them in a reserve or below the line. (What may be expensed or capitalized on a tax return is a question for your accountant, not for a deal sheet.)

Cap rate is annual NOI divided by price. It is useful because it ignores financing: you can compare two buildings as if they were paid in cash. It is also easy to flatter. A cap rate on purchase price can look fine while a cap rate on project cost — price plus rehab — does not. If you are renovating, look at both.

Cap rate is not cash-on-cash return. Cap rate leaves the loan out. Cash-on-cash puts your actual cash, and the mortgage payment, back in. A deal can have a tidy cap rate and still be tight once you service the debt.

How to calculate cash-on-cash return

Cash-on-cash return is year-1 (or annual) pre-tax cash flow divided by total cash invested, expressed as a percentage.

Cash flow starts from NOI and then subtracts the mortgage payment — principal and interest. Be explicit about reserves. Cash flow before reserves can look comfortable. Cash flow after a vacancy-aware operating picture and a capital-expenditure reserve is the one that tells you whether you will write a personal check when the water heater fails. Many weak beginner sheets skip that reserve and then wonder why the first year did not match the model.

In a spreadsheet the idea is:

  • Annual cash flow after reserves ÷ total cash invested
  • Format the result as a percentage
  • Do not mix a monthly cash-flow figure with an annual denominator

There is no universal “good” cash-on-cash number. Markets, rates, and how conservative your vacancy and reserves are will move it. The useful test is whether the return still holds when your assumptions are a little wrong, not whether a blog called 8% strong.

DSCR, break-even occupancy, and the quick screens

Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) is NOI divided by annual debt service: does operating income cover the mortgage, with any room to spare? A common reference point is 1.20, but lenders set their own rules, and a template is not a loan offer. Treat a DSCR below 1.20, or negative cash flow, as a warning light, not a verdict. They tell you to look again.

Break-even occupancy asks how full the property has to stay before operations no longer cover the costs you have modeled, including the mortgage. Next to DSCR, it is the number that keeps you honest in a softer rental market.

Two other quick screens show up on almost every small-investor sheet:

  • Gross rent multiplier (GRM): price divided by gross rent. A coarse comparison tool, not a complete analysis.
  • The 1% rule: monthly rent compared with purchase price, as a first glance only. It is a filter for whether a listing is worth a closer look. It is not a reason to buy, and in many markets almost nothing will pass it.

If a deal only “works” on the 1% rule and falls apart once you enter taxes, insurance, vacancy, and reserves, it did not work.

Stress the vacancy and the rate

A base case is not an analysis. Recalculate cash-on-cash and DSCR if vacancy is worse than you assumed, or if the interest rate is wrong. That can be a small grid, not a second workbook: one axis for vacancy, one for the rate, with cash-on-cash and DSCR in the cells.

If the deal is only acceptable when every assumption is optimistic, you do not have a margin. You have a story. Underwrite the next door as if you do not already love the street.

Be skeptical of the five-year movie

A five-year projection — straight-line growth, a sale based on year-5 NOI, an IRR and an equity multiple — can be a useful sketch. It is also easy to flatter. Expenses often outrun rent; sale costs and tax on a sale are easy to leave out. Do not let a handsome IRR rescue a weak year-1 cash-flow or DSCR picture.

Compare deals side by side, then stop short of a verdict

Once you have the same inputs and the same measures for two or three addresses, a comparison is the point: cash invested, NOI, both cap rates, cash flow before and after reserves, cash-on-cash, DSCR, GRM, the 1% screen, and break-even occupancy. Lights for negative cash flow, a low DSCR, or a going-in cap below the exit cap you are assuming are there to make a weak deal obvious. They are not a purchase recommendation.

When the numbers say wait

A deal that only pencils in the base case, that needs vacancy to be zero, or that depends on an insurance number you have not quoted, is not ready for a bid. There will be another address. The point of the sheet is to make that obvious while walking away is still cheap.
