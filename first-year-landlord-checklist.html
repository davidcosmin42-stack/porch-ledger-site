First year landlord checklist

A first rental year is a sequence, not a personality test.

If you inherited a house, bought a first duplex, or decided to keep a condo as a rental, the useful question is not “am I a landlord now?” It is “what has to happen in what order?” A first year landlord checklist will not make the law the same in every city, and it will not file your taxes. It can keep you from skipping the dull steps that later become a dispute or a year your accountant has to reconstruct.

What follows is a practical twelve-month sequence. Tick a box only when the thing is actually done. Where the law is local — and on deposits, notices, and licensing, it almost always is — the checklist is a reminder to check, not an answer.

1. Before you accept the keys

Before you become responsible for a long-term rental, find out whether you are allowed to rent it in the first place. That may include zoning or occupancy rules, a local rental license or registration, required inspections, HOA or condo restrictions, and habitability or safety standards where the property sits. None of that is the same everywhere. Look it up, or ask a local professional, before you advertise the unit or take a deposit.

Walk the property with a critical list, not a hopeful one: alarms, locks, plumbing, electrical, heat, appliances, and obvious safety problems. A unit that is “mostly ready” is how the first month fills up with complaints. Document the condition as you find it; you will want that record again on move-in day.

2. The lease file you should keep

Keep one file per tenancy, in a place you can actually find in March. Typical contents include the signed lease, any addenda, move-in documentation, the deposit record, ledgers of rent and other charges, notices you sent or received, and later invoices that belong to that unit. If something is only in a text thread, it is not filed.

The lease itself should be a written agreement that fits the property’s jurisdiction. A generic form from the internet is not automatically valid where you are. Have someone qualified review it if you are not sure. This guide will not draft a lease for you.

3. Move-in day

On move-in day, go slowly. Walk the unit with the tenant if you can. Take dated photos and, if you use one, a written condition checklist. Note what is already worn, stained, or missing. If the tenant will sign that checklist, so much the better. Without a record of how the unit looked at the start, a deposit discussion at the end is mostly memory.

Collect only what the lease and local law allow at the start — often rent and a deposit, sometimes other lawful amounts — and receipt them clearly. Put the deposit where you will not spend it as if it were rent. Then hand over keys only when the paperwork you meant to have is actually in the file.

4. The monthly rhythm

The year stays calm if the month is boring. On due day, or the day after any grace period in the lease, you should be able to see who was supposed to pay and who did. Log each receipt the day it lands, one row per payment, including the period it covers. A March bank deposit can still belong to February. That is how a rent roll works: expected versus collected, by unit and by month.

Log expenses the same week they happen, with the property on the receipt or in the file name. If you drove to the property, write down the mileage the same day, using the rate your advisor says applies that year. Once a month, match the books to the bank and look at occupancy, unpaid rent, and leases ending in the next 90 days.

5. Security deposits (general principles only)

A security deposit is money you are holding for someone else. It is not rent, and it should not land on the income line when the tenant moves in. Keep a running record of what you still hold, for which unit, and any deduction you later make.

That is the bookkeeping principle. The legal rules — whether you must use a particular account, whether interest is due, what you may deduct, deadlines, and required notices — are local. Confirm them where the property is, and keep invoices for any deduction. A checklist cannot decide those questions, and neither can a spreadsheet.

6. Maintenance triage, including repair vs. improvement

Decide in advance how a tenant reports a problem, what counts as an emergency, and whom you will call for plumbing, electrical, and heat. You do not need a call center. You need a path that does not start from scratch at 2 a.m.

When the invoice arrives, separate ordinary repairs from capital improvements as best you can, and ask your accountant when you are unsure. A service call that fixes the dishwasher you already had is often an operating expense. A replacement dishwasher, or a new HVAC system, is often a capital improvement. Both leave the bank account. Only ordinary operating expenses should shrink this year’s operating profit; improvements still use cash and belong on their own list. The template will not make the tax decision for you.

7. Documents your accountant will want

Do not wait until April to invent a filing system. Through the year, keep:

  • Income and expense records tagged to a property, with categories you can explain
  • Receipts (a photo the same day is fine) in a folder per property per year
  • A capital-improvement list: date, vendor, amount, and what it was
  • Year-end mortgage statements that split interest from principal
  • A mileage log, if you will use one
  • A record of deposits still held, so they are not mistaken for income

Your accountant can work from a category-by-property summary. They should not have to sort a mixed personal card statement. What you may deduct, depreciate, or report — including on a US Schedule E, if that is how you file — is for a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

8. A mid-year checkup

Sometime around mid-year, stop and look. Have insurance or tax bills moved? Which leases end in the next 90 days? Are your expense categories still the ones you actually use? Mid-year is cheap. Reconstructing a messy category in March is not.

9. Year-end close

Year-end is a pack, not a puzzle: totals by category and by property, the capital-improvement list, the interest figure from the loan statement, mileage, deposits still held, and the receipt folder that matches the books. Close the year on purpose, even if the tax appointment is later.

10. When you are ready for door number two

The first year teaches you what the next purchase has to survive: real taxes, a rental insurance quote, vacancy, maintenance, and a reserve for the big replacements. Underwrite the next door as if you do not already love it. If the only way the new property works is if every assumption is optimistic, you are not ready — or the deal is not.
