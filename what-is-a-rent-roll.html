A collections guide for small, long-term landlords

What is a rent roll?

A rent roll is a dated, unit-by-unit list of who lives where, what rent is due, and what has actually been collected. Here is how to keep one in a spreadsheet if you have a handful of doors, not a hundred.

Porch Ledger · What is a rent roll?

This is a bookkeeping guide, not tax, legal, or investment advice. Deposit rules, notice periods, and what you may deduct from a deposit are local law. Confirm them where the property is.

Ask a broker what a rent roll is, and you will hear about a file that comes with a multifamily deal: every unit, the contract rent, the lease dates, and an as-of date, used to test whether the seller’s income story holds up. Ask a landlord with two houses and a condo, and you will hear something plainer: a sheet they open on Monday to see who has paid and who has not.

Those are the same idea at two different scales. A rent roll is a dated, unit-by-unit schedule of occupancy and rent. It is a census of the leases, not a bank statement. It tells you what is supposed to come in, and — if you keep it properly — what has actually come in. It does not, by itself, show repairs, taxes, or whether a deposit is still sitting in a trust account.

What belongs on a small-landlord rent roll

You do not need a lender’s commercial form. You need one row per unit, kept current, with enough columns that Monday’s questions have answers:

Column Why it is there
Property and unit So a duplex is two rows, not one blurred total.
Tenant name, or “vacant” Vacant units stay on the roll. If you delete empty doors, you hide the hole in income.
Lease start and end, and whether it is month-to-month So you can see what is ending in the next 90 days, and so a rollover does not look like a new tenancy you forgot to file.
Monthly rent and due day The expected amount, from the lease, not from last month’s bank deposit.
Deposit held A liability, not income. The roll should show what you still hold, not treat the move-in payment as rent.
Status Occupied, vacant, notice given, or ended — ended rows stay in the year’s history.

That list is the who lives where snapshot. Next to it you need a payment ledger — one row per receipt — and a grid (or a set of SUMIFS totals) that compares expected rent with collected rent for each unit and month. The snapshot without the grid is only a contact list. The grid without the snapshot cannot tell you whether a shortfall is a late tenant or a door that has been empty since June.

The as-of date, and why “current rent” is not a vibe

A rent roll is only meaningful on a stated date. Leases start, end, and go month-to-month. If you update a cell in place and never say when the picture was true, you will not be able to explain a later discrepancy.

In practice, for a small portfolio, you do not reprint a formal report every week. You keep the lease list accurate, you add payment rows the day money arrives, and you let the month grid be the living report. When someone asks “what does the building collect today?” the answer should come from that dated list, not from memory.

Expected versus collected is the actual job

Expected rent is the lease amount for the period, for units that were supposed to be occupied. Collected rent is the sum of ledger rows tagged to that unit and period. The difference is the only arrears figure worth looking at.

A few things will throw that difference off if you are not careful:

  • A payment that arrives in the wrong calendar month. A 12 March deposit can still be February rent. Put the period on the payment row, not only the bank date.
  • A partial payment. Log what arrived. The shortfall should stay visible until the rest shows up, or until you make a lawful decision about it.
  • A late fee. A fee can make a month look “paid in full” if you lump it in with rent. Keep the fee on its own row, in its own category, so the delay is still visible.
  • A prepaid month. If someone pays February and March together, two period tags (or two rows) keep March from looking like a gift later.

This is also why a bank statement is not a rent roll. The statement proves money moved. It does not tell you which period was paid, whether a late fee was in the total, or that Unit B has been vacant and therefore should not be in the “expected” column at all.

Ended leases and month-to-month tenancies still belong on the roll

When a tenant leaves in June, do not delete the row. The year still happened. If you erase ended leases, your annual collected-rent total will no longer tie to the bank, and you will lose the record of the deposit you returned or deducted. Mark the status as ended, keep the end date, and leave the history in the file.

Month-to-month tenancies are easy to lose in a sheet that only thinks in fixed terms. Give them a clear status and a reminder to review notice dates under the law where the property is. A template cannot know your local notice period.

Deposits and vacancy sit beside the rent, not inside it

A security deposit is money you are holding for someone else. It should not hit the income line at move-in, and it should not be used to plug a month that looks short. Keep a running figure of what you still hold, for which unit. Local law governs deadlines, lawful deductions, interest, and where the money must sit. Confirm those rules; do not ask a spreadsheet to invent them.

Vacancy is the other number people hide by accident. If you only list occupied units, a quiet month looks like a collections problem or, worse, like a fully rented building. Leave vacant units on the roll, and if you want to see the cost of an empty month, keep a simple vacancy log: the dates the door was empty, the rent you did not collect, and any make-ready costs. That is not a tax conclusion. It is a way to see whether a turnover is getting expensive.

How to keep a rent roll in a spreadsheet

You can build a serviceable version in Excel or Google Sheets with three small tables:

  1. Units and leases. One row per unit with the columns in the table above. Use data validation so property names and statuses stay consistent.
  2. Payments. One row per receipt: date, unit, amount, category (rent, late fee, other), and the period the payment covers.
  3. A month grid. Down the side, each unit. Across the top, the months of the year. Each cell is a formula that sums payment rows for that unit and period, sitting next to the expected rent from the lease list. Conditional formatting can flag a shortfall without you scanning every cell.

A typical collected-rent cell is a SUMIFS (or Sheets SUMIFS) over the payment ledger: sum amount, where unit matches, period matches, and category is rent. Do not type the monthly total by hand. Typed totals go stale the first time you add a late payment.

Once a week, on a fixed day, open the grid, look at shortfalls, and look at leases ending in the next 90 days. That is the whole operating rhythm. The work is not designing a prettier dashboard. The work is adding the payment row the day the money arrives.

If you also need a year-end income-and-expense picture — repairs versus improvements, interest versus principal, a pack for your accountant — that is a separate job from the rent roll. How to track rental income and expenses in a spreadsheet covers that ledger.

Mistakes that make a clean-looking roll unreliable

  • Deleting vacant or ended units so the sheet “looks occupied.”
  • Using the bank date as the only date, with no period tag.
  • Folding late fees, pet rent, or a deposit into the rent cell.
  • Overwriting last year’s figures in place instead of starting a new year (or a new tab) and keeping the history.
  • Treating the rent roll as proof of income for a purchase or a refinance without also looking at what was actually collected, and at expenses. Analyzing a rent roll for a deal means testing the leases against collections and against a real expense picture. A handsome contract-rent total is not cash in the bank.
