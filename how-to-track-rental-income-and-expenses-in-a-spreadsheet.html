How to track rental income and expenses in a spreadsheet

A calm system for landlords with one to eight doors: one row per payment or expense, a rent roll that shows who is behind, and a year-end pack an accountant can actually use.

This is a bookkeeping guide, not tax, legal, or investment advice. Deposit rules, notice periods, and what may be expensed, depreciated, or deducted are for a qualified professional in the property’s jurisdiction — including if you are outside the US.

You can run two or three rental doors in a spreadsheet. You cannot run them in a group chat and a shoebox of receipts.

A lot of first-year landlords bounce between two extremes: software built for dozens of units, or a template so large they abandon it after a week. If you have one to eight long-term rentals, the job is smaller than that. You need a spreadsheet you will actually open, a weekly habit, and a year-end pack an accountant can use without reconstructing your year from the bank feed.

This guide walks through that system — how to track rental income and expenses in a spreadsheet, whether you work in Excel, Google Sheets, or LibreOffice Calc.

What the books actually have to do

In the first year, the books have four jobs:

  • Collections: who was supposed to pay, and who did.
  • Liabilities: security deposits you are holding, which are not income.
  • Operations: vacancy, repairs, insurance, taxes, and the other ordinary costs of keeping the doors open.
  • A year-end pack: a category-by-property summary you can sit down with an accountant, plus the receipts that support it.

If a tab, app, or habit does not serve one of those jobs, you probably do not need it yet.

Build the ledger as one row per movement

The structure that survives a messy year is one row every time money arrives or leaves — not one column per category, and not a single total typed at the end of the month. When a tenant pays, add a row. When you buy a faucet, add a row. When the insurer drafts the premium, add a row. Monthly totals then become formulas, which means they can be checked.

A practical set of columns looks like this:

Column What you type
Date The day the money actually moved, not the day you remembered to log it.
Property or unit Which door it belongs to. Use the same name every time, ideally from a drop-down.
Type Income, operating expense, capital improvement, mortgage interest, or principal. More on those distinctions below.
Category A short, stable list: rent, late fee, insurance, repairs, supplies, property tax, and so on. Do not invent a new spelling each month.
Amount A positive number. The Type column tells the sheet whether the amount came in or went out.
Period For rent only: which month the payment is for. A 12 March bank deposit can still belong to February.
Note or vendor Anything you will want at year-end: invoice number, “boiler service,” “March rent, paid late.”

Keep the category list on its own small sheet and point the ledger at it with data validation, so you pick a category instead of retyping it. Summaries that use SUMIFS (or the Sheets equivalent) can then add up rent for a unit, repairs for a property, or insurance for the year without you touching a calculator.

Record rent when it arrives, dated the day it arrived. That is the cash basis, and for many small landlords it is the simplest method. Whether it is the method you should use for a tax return is a question for your advisor; the ledger’s job is to hold what actually happened.

Excel, Google Sheets, or Calc

Any of the three will do this job. Excel is still the most predictable place for drop-downs, named ranges, and printed year-end pages. Google Sheets is useful if you want the file on your phone when a receipt lands, or if a bookkeeper needs a link instead of an email attachment. LibreOffice Calc is a solid free desktop option. If you move a file between them, re-check the drop-downs and any lookup formulas after import. Number formats that ship as US dollars can be changed if you work in another currency.

What you should not do is split the year across tools: some rent in a notes app, some expenses in a banking app’s “tags,” and a spreadsheet that only exists in December. Pick one ledger and let the bank statements be the thing you reconcile against, not a second set of books.

A weekly and monthly rhythm

The spreadsheet only works if you touch it on a schedule.

On due day, or the day after any grace period in the lease, glance at who has paid. Log each rent receipt the day it lands — one row per payment, with the period it covers. That is how you see who is actually behind, not just what hit the account.

Log expenses the day they happen, not at month-end. Write the property address on the receipt, photograph it, and drop the photo into a folder for that property and year. If you drove to the property, record the mileage the same day, using the rate your advisor says applies that year.

Once a month, reconcile the spreadsheet to the bank and card statements. Fifteen minutes is enough when the month is clean. Then look at four numbers: occupancy, unpaid rent, leases ending in the next 90 days, and whether any repair is starting to look like a replacement.

Keep a rent roll next to the ledger

The ledger tells you what moved. It does not, by itself, tell you what was supposed to move. That second view is the rent roll: expected versus collected, by unit and by month.

Expected rent comes from the lease: the amount, the day it is due, and whether the tenancy is still active, month-to-month, or already ended. Ended leases still belong in the year’s history. If you delete them, your annual collected-rent figure will not match the bank.

A late fee can make a month look “paid in full” even if the rent itself arrived late. Keep the fee on its own row so the delay stays visible. For a fuller walk through the rent roll itself — what the phrase means, which columns to keep, and the mistakes that hide arrears — see What is a rent roll?

Deposits are a liability

A security deposit is money you are holding for someone else. It is not rent, and it should not land on the income line when the tenant moves in — or quietly stay there when they move out.

Keep a running ledger of what you still hold, for which unit, and any lawful deduction you later make. Local law governs deadlines, what you can deduct, whether interest is due, and where the money must sit. No spreadsheet can decide that for you. Confirm the rules where the property is, and keep invoices for any deduction.

Repairs, improvements, interest, and principal

Two distinctions do most of the year-end work.

The first is repair versus capital improvement. A modest service call that fixes the dishwasher you already had is usually an ordinary operating expense. Replacing the appliance, or installing a new HVAC system, is often a capital improvement. Both leave the bank account. Only ordinary operating expenses should shrink this year’s operating profit. Capital improvements still use cash, so they belong on a separate list, below the line. Which side of the line a given invoice falls on is a question for your accountant, not for a template.

The second is mortgage interest versus principal. A loan payment is usually two things in one debit. The interest is the cost of borrowing. The principal is you paying down the loan — a transfer from your bank account into equity in the building, not an operating expense. If you log the whole payment as an expense, your “profit” figure understates how the property actually runs, and a tax summary can overstate deductions. Your lender’s year-end statement tells you the split. Until it arrives, it is safer to under-state interest than to guess high.

Net operating income is income minus ordinary operating expenses, including mortgage interest and business mileage. It does not include loan principal. A year can show healthy net operating income and still feel tight in cash, especially if you also paid for a capital improvement. That is not a spreadsheet error. It is the difference between profit and cash.

Mileage belongs in its own log: date, destination, purpose, and miles. Official rates change by year and country. Enter the one your advisor says applies to you.

A mid-year checkup and a year-end close

Mid-year, look at insurance and tax bills that have moved, leases in that 90-day window, and whether your expense categories still match how you actually spend. It is cheaper to fix a messy category in July than to reconstruct it in March.

At year-end, the goal is a pack, not a puzzle. Typical contents:

  • Income and expense totals by category and by property
  • A list of capital improvements (date, vendor, amount, what it was)
  • Mortgage interest as stated on the year-end loan statement, separate from principal
  • A mileage total
  • Security deposits still held, so they are not mistaken for income
  • The receipt folder that matches the spreadsheet

Your accountant can work from a category-by-property rollup. They should not have to sort a year of mixed personal and rental charges. If you are in the US, some templates offer optional Schedule E–style category hints so the rollup is easier to talk through. Hints are not a filing instruction, and a spreadsheet should not compute depreciation or file a return for you.

When to stop using a spreadsheet

A handful of long-term doors is a good fit for a spreadsheet. Dozens of units, partners who need tenant portals and automatic reminders, or a bookkeeper who wants a full accounting system is software territory.

The point of keeping the file small is not to pretend a spreadsheet is a property-management company. If you have outgrown a spreadsheet, you have outgrown this approach. That is useful information, not a failure of the tools.

Until then, the work is ordinary and repeatable: log income and expenses when they happen, keep deposits off the income line, separate repairs from improvements, split interest from principal, and hand your accountant a year that already makes sense.
